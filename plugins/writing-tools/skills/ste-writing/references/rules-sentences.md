# STE Part 1 — Sections 4–6: Sentences, Procedural writing, Descriptive writing

## Section 4 — Sentences (general rules for both writing types)

STE has different sentence rules for procedures (Section 5) and descriptive text (Section 6).
Section 4 gives the general rules that apply to both types of writing.

### Rule 4.1 — Write short and clear sentences.

- In procedures, give short, clear instructions directly to the reader (imperative form).
- In descriptive text, make sure each sentence has only one topic (subject or idea) and does not
  contain the imperative form. Then, in the sentences that follow, gradually give more information
  about that topic.
- Never be abstract. Make sure the text clearly shows how to do a task or how a system operates.
- Be accurate. Do not give information that is not accurate or can have different meanings.

Examples:

- Non-STE: "To remove the cover assembly (9), first remove the four screws (10) that attach the
  cover (11) to the housing (12), and then, after taking the cover (11) off the housing (12),
  remove the preformed packing (13) and throw it away."
- STE:
  "1. Remove the cover assembly (9) as follows:
      A. Remove the four screws (10) that attach the cover (11) to the housing (12).
      B. Remove the cover (11) from the housing (12).
      C. Remove and discard the preformed packing (13)."

- Non-STE: "No leaks are permitted." (does not tell the reader the action)
- STE: "Make sure that there are no leaks."

- Non-STE: "Different temperatures will change the cure time." (abstract — how does it change?)
- STE: "When the temperature increases, the cure time will decrease."
  More accurate still: "The cure time is 2 hours at a temperature of 20 °C."

### Rule 4.2 — Do not omit words or use contractions to make your sentences shorter.

Each sentence must have all its parts. Omission makes a sentence shorter but not easier to read.
Write all words in full. Specifically:

- Do not omit nouns:
  - Non-STE: "Can be a maximum of five inches long."
  - STE: "Cracks can have a maximum length of five inches."
- Do not omit verbs:
  - Non-STE: "Rotary switch to INPUT."
  - STE: "Set the rotary switch to INPUT."
- Do not omit the subject:
  - Non-STE: "If installed, remove the shims."
  - STE: "If shims are installed, remove them."
  - In a warning: "IF NOT, THIS CAN CAUSE INJURY TO PERSONS." →
    "A PRESSURIZED SYSTEM CAN CAUSE INJURY TO PERSONS."
- Do not omit articles (omitted articles cause ambiguity):
  - Non-STE: "Remove the bolt and stop."
  - STE: "Remove the bolt and the stop."
- Do not use contractions ("don't," "isn't," "aren't"):
  - Non-STE: "If your hands are wet, don't touch the USB power adapter."
  - STE: "If your hands are wet, do not touch the USB power adapter."

### Rule 4.3 — Use a vertical list for complex texts.

When a sentence is long and must include many different items (for example, components, parts,
or documents) or actions, put them in a vertical list.

Rules for making a vertical list:
- Put a colon (:) at the end of the first sentence, before the first item in the list.
- Identify each item with a number, letter, punctuation mark, or symbol — for example:
  a dash (-), a bullet point (•), a letter (a, b, c…), or a number (1, 2, 3…).
  For which marks or symbols to use, refer to the applicable specifications for technical
  publications, style guides, and other official directives.
- Start each item with an uppercase letter.
- Where applicable, use an article before the noun that is the subject of each item.
- Put a period at the end of an item if it is a full sentence.
- Do not put a period at the end of an item if it is not a full sentence. (This applies even if
  the item contains a verb, e.g. "One check valve that prevents the backflow of fluid".)
- Do not put a comma or a semicolon at the end of an item.
- Put a period at the end of the last item in the list.

Additional mechanics:
- You can use vertical lists in procedural and descriptive writing, but you cannot mix the two
  types of writing in the same vertical list.
- In safety instructions, include the negative command (DO NOT) where necessary for each item.
- Make sure each item connects clearly and grammatically to the lead-in text before the colon.
- Keep the layout easy to read — use the same level for all items. Do not nest a second vertical
  list inside the primary list; fold sub-items into a parenthetical instead:
  "The flange (2) (that includes the two O-rings (6) and the seals (7))".

Examples:

- Non-STE: "The wheel assembly comprises the tire, the tube, the spokes, the spoke fittings,
  the valve, and the hub."
- STE:
  "The wheel assembly has these parts:
      - The tire
      - The tube
      - The spokes
      - The spoke fittings
      - The valve
      - The hub."

- Non-STE: "If the RAT is retracted, remove the tag, open the isolating valves and, ensuring that
  there is no possibility of fouling, slowly extend the RAT to its full stroke. Close the
  isolating valves; tag 'DO NOT OPERATE.' Release the DOWN pressure."
- STE:
  "If the Ram Air Turbine (RAT) is retracted:
      (a) Remove the tag.
      (b) Open the isolating valves.
      (c) Slowly and fully extend the RAT and make sure that it does not touch other parts.
      (d) Close the isolating valves.
      (e) Tag the isolating valves with 'DO NOT OPERATE.'
      (f) Release the DOWN pressure."

- Non-STE (safety list): "…DO NOT: - PUT YOUR FEET ON THE APU LINE. - USE THE APU LINE AS A HANDLE."
- STE: "…: - DO NOT PUT YOUR FEET ON THE APU LINE. - DO NOT USE THE APU LINE AS A HANDLE."

### Rule 4.4 — Use connecting words and connecting phrases to connect sentences that contain related topics.

- Connecting words and phrases connect a topic in one sentence with an idea in the sentence that
  follows. In descriptive text they give the writing a logical structure.
- Connecting words approved in the dictionary include: "and," "but," "then," "thus."
- Example connecting phrases: "as a result," "at the same time."
- You can also use demonstrative adjectives (this, these) as connecting words to connect ideas in
  related sentences.
- In procedures, use connecting words when an explanation is necessary after a work step, and in
  safety instructions to connect related sentences or make the text clear.

Examples in STE:
- "If the pressure increases, it changes the electrical currents in the transmitter. Thus, the
  power unit supplies currents to the indicator on the related panel."
- "When the hydraulic pressure is released, the sequence valve moves to the open position.
  As a result, the actuators are connected to the hydraulic return."
- "Tag the disassembled parts. This method will help you during the subsequent procedures."

### Rule 4.5 — When applicable, use an article (the, a, an) or a demonstrative adjective (this, these) before a noun or a multi-word noun.

- Articles and demonstrative adjectives show the position of nouns and multi-word nouns in the
  sentence. Use them correctly and do not omit them to make the text shorter:
  - Non-STE: "Turn shaft assembly." → STE: "Turn the shaft assembly."
  - Non-STE: "Data module tells you how to operate unit."
    → STE: "This data module tells you how to operate the unit."
- Exception — do not use articles in general statements or concepts:
  - "Solvents can cause damage to paint." (no specified solvent or paint)
  - "The machine operates with high efficiency." (general concept)
  - "This software increases performance." (abstract quality)
- In short sentences, it can be clearer to use articles before all nouns:
  "Install the nuts (2) and the bolts (3)."
- In a long series of items, use the article only before the first noun:
  "Discard the O-rings (3), gaskets (4), seals (7), and washers (9)."
- Article placement controls adjective scope in a series:
  - "Install the new O-rings (15), spacers (14), nut (13), and safety pin (12)."
    (one article: all items in the series are new)
  - "Install the new O-rings (15), the spacers (14), the nut (13), and the safety pin (12)."
    (repeated articles: only the O-rings are new)
- No definite article before a noun followed by an alphanumeric identifier (the identifier shows
  it is a proper noun):
  - Incorrect: "Tag the circuit breaker 36L7." → Correct: "Tag circuit breaker 36L7."

## Section 5 — Procedural writing

### Rule 5.1 — Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 20 words in each sentence.

- Maximum sentence length in procedures: 20 words.
- Warnings, cautions, and other safety instructions must also obey this rule.
- Exception: notes (rule 5.5) do not obey rule 5.1 — the maximum length of a sentence in a note
  is 25 words (notes contain information only).
- Divide a long instruction into shorter sentences. Keep the sentences in the same work step when
  the actions occur at the same time.

Example:
- Non-STE: "Put preservation oil into the unit through the vent hole until the oil level is
  approximately 6 mm (0.24 inches) below the surface of the flange cover." (25 words)
- STE: "Put preservation oil into the unit through the vent hole. (10 words) Continue until the
  oil level is approximately 6 mm (0.24 in) below the surface of the flange cover." (16 words)
  (One work step — the two actions occur at the same time, so they cannot be separate steps.)

### Rule 5.2 — Write only one instruction in each sentence unless two or more actions occur at the same time.

- Write one instruction per sentence and clearly show the sequence of work steps (usually with
  numbers or letters). You can use as many work steps as you want in a procedure.
- More than one instruction in a sentence is permitted only when the actions occur at the same
  time:
  - "Hold the panel in its open position and install the fastener."
  - "Cut and remove the wire." / "Remove and discard the seal."
- More than one sentence in a work step is permitted:
  - When actions occur at the same time
  - When a result occurs immediately after an action:
    "Measure the leakage from the outlet port. The leakage must not be more than 0.5 cc/minute."

Example:
- Non-STE: "Set the TEST switch to the middle position and release the SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST switch."
  (the two actions do not occur at the same time)
- STE:
  "A. Set the TEST switch to the middle position.
   B. Release the SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST switch."

### Rule 5.3 — Write instructions in the imperative (command) form.

- The imperative form gives the reader a clear instruction. Other sentence structures cause
  ambiguity — the reader will not know:
  - If it is important to do a work step
  - If a different person did the work step
  - If a different person must do the work step in the future.
- Do not use "must" before the imperative form, unless the instruction is very important for
  safety (for example, in a safety instruction) or when you give an important condition:
  "WARNING: IF YOU MUST CUT THE WIRE, ALWAYS USE A PROTECTIVE MASK."

Examples:
- Non-STE: "The test can be continued." → STE: "Continue the test."
- Non-STE: "Oil and grease are to be removed with a degreasing agent."
  → STE: "Remove oil and grease with a degreasing agent."
- Do not write: "Before you remove the clamp, you must disconnect the hose."
  Write: "Before you remove the clamp, disconnect the hose."

### Rule 5.4 — When there is a condition that the reader must know about first, start the instruction with a descriptive statement. Then, divide that descriptive statement from the command with a comma.

- If a special condition is necessary for a work step, the reader must know it first. Write the
  condition first, then a comma to show the end of the condition and the start of the instruction.
- The comma position can change the meaning of the sentence — place it carefully:
  - "If the Constant Speed Drive (CSD) does not operate correctly, disconnect it from the
    gearbox." ("correctly" modifies "operate")
  - "If the Constant Speed Drive (CSD) does not operate, correctly disconnect it from the
    gearbox." ("correctly" modifies "disconnect")

Examples:
- Do not write: "Set the switch to NORMAL when the light comes on."
  Write: "When the light comes on, set the switch to NORMAL."
- Do not write: "Apply the primer when the surface is dry."
  Write: "When the surface is dry, apply the primer."

### Rule 5.5 — Write notes only to give information, not instructions.

- Notes only give information to help the reader during a procedure. They contain descriptive
  text and obey the rules for descriptive writing.
- Notes must not give instructions, requirements, or limits.
- A note can have one or more sentences; each sentence in a note has a maximum of 25 words.
- Do not use the imperative form in a note — the note becomes an instruction. Move such text into
  a numbered work step instead.
- If information in a note is important to prevent damage or injury, give it in a safety
  instruction (WARNING/CAUTION) — even if the note text has no imperative form.
- Do not use a note for limits, tolerances, or results of a work step. That information must come
  directly after the related action in the work step.
- Test for correct use of notes: read the procedure without the notes and make sure the reader
  can do it correctly without them. If important information is only in a note: remove it from
  the note, write it as a work step in the applicable place, and do the test again.
- Notes belong in procedures. In descriptions, use notes only if they are necessary for
  illustrations or tables that are parts of the description.

Examples:
- STE: "NOTE: The gyroscope will become stable after approximately 15 seconds."
- Non-STE: "NOTE: Make sure that the avionics ventilation system continues to operate correctly."
  (imperative — not a note)
  → STE: "(6) Make sure that the avionics ventilation system continues to operate correctly."
  (a work step in the procedure)
- Non-STE: "B. Measure the leakage from the outlet port.
            NOTE: The leakage must not be more than 0.5 cc/minute." (limit in a note)
  → STE: "B. Measure the leakage from the outlet port. The leakage must not be more than
  0.5 cc/minute." (action first, then the related limit, in the work step)

## Section 6 — Descriptive writing

Descriptive writing gives information, not instructions — the imperative form of the verb is not
permitted. Descriptive writing can be:
- A description of an item, product, system, or component — its function, how it is made, and
  how it operates
- A text that gives general information (for example, reports, brochures, and papers)
- A note in a procedure.

### Rule 6.1 — Give information gradually.

- Give information gradually and make sure that each sentence contains only one subject.
- If you give too much information too quickly, the text is hard to understand and the reader
  must read it again.
- Introduce the topic/system first, then add details sentence by sentence in logical paragraphs.

Example (condensed):
- Non-STE: "During the approach to the runway, deviation pointers in the course indicators give
  commands to fly up or down and left or right. This information comes from the VHF transceivers,
  which are part of the Instrument Landing System. …"
  (details before the topic; multiple subjects per sentence)
- STE: "The Instrument Landing System (the system) on the aircraft shows data that helps the
  pilot during the approach to the runway. This system shows the pilot the deviations from the
  localizer course and the glideslope path. The localizer course aligns with the centerline of
  the runway. And the glideslope path is at a constant angle to the threshold of the runway. …"
  (topic first; one subject per sentence)

### Rule 6.2 — Use key words and key phrases to give your text a logical structure.

- Key words and key phrases occur repeatedly in a text to connect different ideas and show how
  information is related.
- Also use connecting words and connecting phrases ("and," "but," "then," "thus"; "as a result,"
  "at the same time"). They work like traffic signs — they tell the reader if the information is
  new, different, or a result of previous information.
- Do not change key words or key phrases in your text. The same terminology keeps the text clear
  and correct; different terminology makes the text hard to read.

Example mechanic (from the ILS text of rule 6.1):
- Sentence 2 repeats the key words "system," "show," and "pilot" from sentence 1 to add more
  information.
- Sentences 3 and 4 repeat "localizer course" and "glideslope path" and add new information.
- Sentence 5 repeats "runway" and the key phrase "during the approach to the runway" to refer
  back to sentence 1 and give more information about it.

### Rule 6.3 — Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 25 words in each sentence.

- Maximum sentence length in descriptive writing: 25 words (procedures allow only 20; descriptive
  text is more complex than procedural text).
- Divide long sentences into shorter ones.

Example:
- Non-STE: "A smartphone is a cellular telephone that has an integrated computer and many other
  functions, such as an operating system, internet browsing as well as the ability to run
  software applications." (31 words)
- STE: "A smartphone is a cellular telephone that has an integrated computer and many other
  functions. (15 words) It includes an operating system and an internet browser, and it can also
  operate software applications. (16 words)"

### Rule 6.4 — Use paragraphs to show related information.

- In descriptive writing, paragraphs keep related information together and give a logical
  sequence to the text (as numbers and letters do for work steps in procedures).
- Each paragraph starts with a "topic sentence" that tells the reader the topic of that
  paragraph. The sentences that follow explain it or give more information related to it.
- When a new paragraph starts, the reader knows there will be a new topic or different
  information in that paragraph.

Example pattern (lightning strike description):
- Paragraph (1): topic sentence "A lightning strike can cause damage to the aircraft," then
  details and a vertical list of the attachment points.
- Paragraph (2): what happens from the attachment points (current path, possible malfunctions).
- Paragraph (3): the necessary inspection before the next flight.

### Rule 6.5 — Make sure that each paragraph has only one topic.

- The topic sentence is the first and most important sentence in a paragraph. It gives new
  information and makes a logical connection between the new information and previous
  information — it usually contains a key word and/or a connecting word or connecting phrase.
- From the topic sentences, the reader understands the contents and finds information quickly.
  The topic sentences of a text, written down in order, make a good outline of its content.
- The other sentences in each paragraph give the information a logical structure and add more
  information on the topic of the paragraph.

Example: the ILS text divides into two one-topic paragraphs:
- Paragraph 1 (sentences 1–5) — topic: "Data to help the pilot."
- Paragraph 2 (sentences 6–7) — topic: "How this data is transmitted."

### Rule 6.6 — Make sure that no paragraph has more than six sentences.

- Maximum paragraph length: six sentences. If a paragraph has more than six sentences, divide it
  into two smaller paragraphs.
- Do not put different topics in the same paragraph.
- Short paragraphs divide the text into logical units and keep the reader's attention; too-long
  paragraphs lose this function.

Example pattern (fuel manifold description): six consecutive one-topic paragraphs of 1, 2, 5, 4,
4, and 2 sentences — one paragraph each for the manifold's primary parts, the valve body, leakage
prevention, the actuator assembly, the pressure transducer, and the fittings.
