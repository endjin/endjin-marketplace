# STE Part 1 — Sections 1–3: Words, Multi-word nouns, Verbs

## Section 1 — Words

### Rule 1.1 — Use words that are: approved in the dictionary, technical nouns, or technical verbs.
- STE has a controlled dictionary (Part 2) of the words most frequently used in technical writing.
- Words not in the dictionary are usable only if they fit the specified categories of technical nouns (Rule 1.5) or technical verbs (Rule 1.12).
- A technical noun/verb is a term that refers to a specified concept or process applicable to a subject field; these usually live in your company glossary or terminology database.
- The dictionary also lists unapproved words with approved alternatives.

### Rule 1.2 — Use approved words from the dictionary only as the specified part of speech.
- Each dictionary word has a specified part of speech; use it only in that role.
- Some words are approved as more than one part of speech (e.g. "clean" is verb and adjective); sentence position shows the function.
- If the approved alternative has a different part of speech, restructure the sentence. Never let a replacement change meaning.
- If a word is not in the dictionary: find its best approved synonym, or rewrite with other approved words.

Examples ("test" is approved noun, not verb; "dim" is approved adjective, not verb):
- Non-STE: Test the system for leaks. → STE: Do the leak test of the system.
- Non-STE: Dim the lights. → STE: Set the lights to the dim position.
- Non-STE: Make sure that the valve is operable. → STE: Make sure that the valve can operate. ("operable" not approved; alternative is verb "operate")

### Rule 1.3 — Use approved words only with their approved meanings.
- Each approved word has one specified approved meaning, often more restricted than in standard English.
- Example: "follow" is approved only as "come after, go after" — not "obey".

Example:
- Non-STE: Follow the safety instructions. → STE: Obey the safety instructions.
- STE (approved meaning): Do the procedures that follow:

### Rule 1.4 — Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives.
- The dictionary lists each approved verb with its approved forms, e.g. REMOVE (v), REMOVES, REMOVED, REMOVED = infinitive/imperative, simple present, simple past, past participle (as adjective).
- Adjectives appear in base form with comparative/superlative in parentheses where applicable, e.g. SLOW (SLOWER, SLOWEST).
- Adjectives that form comparative/superlative with "more/most" have no listed forms — "more" and "most" are themselves approved words.

### Rule 1.5 — You can use words that you can include in a technical noun category.
- The dictionary contains no technical nouns (too many; each subject field differs). Use your company glossary/terminology database.
- A word is usable as a technical noun if it fits one or more of these 22 categories (listed words are examples only, not a complete list):

1. **Official parts information** — design items in parts catalogs or engineering drawings: bolt, cable, clip, engine, filter, oil seal, propeller, screw, switch, transceiver
2. **Vehicles or machines, and locations on them**: aircraft, bicycle, cabin, cargo compartment, cockpit, fuselage, helicopter, ship, train, wing root
3. **Tools and support equipment, their parts, and locations on them**: access ladder, blade, clamp, drill, gauge, jack, test rig, torque wrench, trestle
4. **Materials, consumables, and unwanted material**: acid, adhesive, debris, dirt, fuel, grease, oil, paint, sealant, stainless steel, waste, water, wire
5. **Facilities, infrastructure, and logistic procedures**: airport, base, building, dock, gate, hangar, packaging, port, shipping, storage, transport
6. **Systems, components and circuits, their functions, configurations, and parts**: air conditioning, exhaust, hardware, inlet, latch, pump, reverse mode, standby mode, upright position
7. **Mathematical, scientific, engineering terms, and formulas**: acceleration, capacitance, coefficient, curve, density, diameter, failure, pressure, ratio, torque, voltage, "C = (A - B) - 0.063 mm"
8. **Navigation and geographic terms**: air, altitude, attitude, axis, bank, clearance, climb, coordinates, datum, drag, east, glideslope, heading, landing, north, pitch, roll, skid, France, Lima
9. **Numbers, units of measurement and time (and their symbols)**: 92, ampere (A), half (½), hour (h), kilogram (kg), knot, liter (L), meter (m), minute, second (s), spring, winter, zero
10. **Quoted text** — text you cannot change (placards, labels, signs, markings, displays): EXIT sign, ON position, FAULT legend, NO STEP marking, FASTEN SAFETY BELT sign
11. **Professional roles, individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities**: air traffic control, captain, crew, manufacturer, operator, EASA, FAA, USA
12. **Parts of the body**: blood, ear, eyes, hand, head, lung, mouth, respiratory tract, skin
13. **Common personal effects, food, and beverages**: clothing, coffee, footwear, jewelry, matches, milk, perfume, shampoo
14. **Medical terms**: allergy, aspirin, asthma, breathing, dizziness, headache, medication, nausea, pulse, virus
15. **Official documents, parts of documentation, standards, and guidelines**: caution, chapter, Checklist, danger, data module, figure, note, page, paragraph, Service Bulletin, table, warning
16. **Environmental and operational conditions**: atmosphere, cloud, day, ice, humidity, lightning, night, rain, snow, storm, turbulence, wind
17. **Colors**: black, gray, green, magenta, orange, red, white, yellow. (Colors are adjectives grammatically, but STE treats them as technical nouns. Comparative/superlative forms — blacker, the reddest — are not permitted.)
18. **Damage terms**: buckle, chafing, corrosion, crack, dent, discoloration, distortion, erosion, fracture, kink, nick, scratch
19. **Computer science, information and communication technology**: AI, backup, backup file, cursor, database, e-mail, firewall, icon, interface, memory, menu, network, screen, software, update
20. **Civil and military operations**: armed forces, bomb, checkpoint, customer support, deployment, ejection seat, end user, gun, lifecycle, mission, OEM, patrol
21. **Law and regulations**: appeal, arbitration, compliance, contract, court, jurisdiction, law, signature, statute, waiver
22. **Animals, plants, and other life forms**: bacteria, bird, cat, conifer, dog, fungi, horse, insect, moss, termite

### Rule 1.6 — Use a word that is not approved in the dictionary, only when it is a technical noun or part of a technical noun.
- An unapproved dictionary word is still usable if it fits an applicable technical noun category in that context.
- The same word can fall into different categories depending on meaning/context (e.g. "base": category 7 in geometry, category 5 as a facility — but not for a surface, where "bottom" is the alternative).
- A word not in the dictionary can also appear as part of a multi-word technical noun (e.g. "relative" in "relative angular position", category 7).
- Do not replace a word inside an approved technical noun with its dictionary alternative ("main landing gear" stays; "primary landing gear" is wrong).

Examples:
- Non-STE: ...spigots at the base of the unit... → STE: ...spigots at the bottom of the unit... (but STE: The base of the triangle is 5 cm.)
- Non-STE: ...one person is available as backup. → STE: Two persons are necessary to do this procedure. (but STE: Do the backup of the computer at regular intervals.)
- Non-STE: The laptop has these main parts: → STE: The laptop has these primary parts: (but STE: Retract the main landing gear.)

### Rule 1.7 — Do not use words that are technical nouns as verbs.
- Use a technical noun only as a noun, or as an adjective inside a different technical noun — never as a verb. Restructure the sentence so the word stays a noun.
- Exception: a word can be both a technical noun and a technical verb if it fits a category under both Rule 1.5 and Rule 1.12 (e.g. "drill": tool noun in "use a carbide drill", manufacturing verb in "Drill a hole").

Examples:
- Non-STE: Oil the steel surfaces. → STE: Apply oil to the steel surfaces.
- Non-STE: If you think it will snow... → STE: If you think that snow will fall...

### Rule 1.8 — Use technical nouns that are approved in your company, industry, or subject field.
- If your company/industry/subject field has an approved technical noun for an item or process, use it (typically found in official parts information and company documentation).

### Rule 1.9 — When you must select a technical noun, use one which is short and easy to understand.
- When no company/industry technical noun exists, select one that is short (not more than three words) and easy to understand.
- If index numbers and illustrations identify the part, a simple noun is sufficient; otherwise add one or two adjectives to help the reader.

Example:
- Non-STE: Remove the four stainless steel pan head machine screws (10) that attach the metallic machined flange (15)... → STE: Remove the four screws (10) that attach the flange (15) to the cover (20).

### Rule 1.10 — Do not use regional, slang, or jargon words as technical nouns.
- Use well-known words; regional terms, technical slang, and jargon are unclear to readers outside a small group.

Examples:
- Non-STE: ...attach a choker to the heavy machinery... → STE: ...attach a cable to the heavy machinery...
- Non-STE: ...do not brick the router. → STE: ...do not set the router to OFF.
- Non-STE: Remove your gear from the work area. → STE: Remove your tools and equipment from the work area.

### Rule 1.11 — Do not use different technical nouns for the same item.
- Pick the approved technical noun for an item and use it consistently throughout the text; never vary between synonyms.

Example:
- Non-STE: "servo control unit" / "actuator" / "control unit" for the same item → STE: "actuator" everywhere.

### Rule 1.12 — You can use verbs that you can include in a technical verb category.
- The dictionary contains no technical verbs; use your company glossary/terminology database. Technical verbs must obey the same rules as approved verbs (Section 3).
- A verb is usable as a technical verb if it fits one or more of these 4 categories (examples only, not complete):

1. **Manufacturing processes**
   - a) Remove material: drill, grind, mill, ream, unsolder
   - b) Add material: flame, insulate, remetal, retread
   - c) Attach material: braze, crimp, solder, weld
   - d) Change mechanical strength, structure, or physical properties: anneal, cure, decay, freeze, heat-treat, magnetize, normalize, vaporize
   - e) Change surface finish: buff, burnish, dress, passivate, plate, polish
   - f) Change shape: blend, cast, extrude, spin, stamp
2. **Computer processes and applications**
   - a) Input/output processes: click, digitize, enter, press, print, swipe, tap, type
   - b) User interface and application processes: clear, close, copy, cut, delete, drag, enable, encrypt, erase, highlight, maximize, minimize, navigate, open, paste, save, scroll, sort, store, validate, zoom in, zoom out
   - c) System operations: abort, boot, debug, download, format, install, load, manage, process, reboot, update, upgrade, upload
3. **Instructions and information for applicable subject fields**
   - a) Engineering, mathematical, scientific: bisect, compensate for, convert, detect, float, modulate, radiate, transform, sink
   - b) Medical: disinfect, intubate, operate, prescribe, sanitize, sterilize
   - c) Civil and military operations: aim, arm, detect, disable, explode, fire, inhibit, intercept, lase, load, lock on, unlatch, unload, parachute
   - d) Navigation: approach, descend, deviate, fly, hover, land, maintain, navigate, retrim, take off, trim, respond, taxi
   - e) Automotive and railway: accelerate, brake, couple, crank, crash, decouple, dispatch, drift, inflate, park, qualify, steer
   - f) Energy, oil, and gas: compress, distill, drill, emit, extract, inject, pump
4. **Law and regulations** (legal/regulatory texts only): acknowledge, comply with, communicate, conform to, describe, enforce, explain, meet (a requirement), inform, modify, notify, omit, regulate, sign, supersede, understand, waive

Constraints on technical verbs:
- If an approved dictionary verb gives the instruction accurately, use it — do not use a technical verb when approved words suffice.
- Use only technical verbs that are correct and specific in your context, not general or unclear ones.
- Do not turn technical nouns into verbs (see Rule 1.7); prefer approved verb + technical noun.
- Some unapproved dictionary words can still be technical verbs in the right category/context.

Examples:
- Non-STE: If you detect broken wires, repair them. → STE: If you find broken wires, repair them. (but STE: The security scanner detects metal objects.)
- Non-STE: Machine the hole until it has a diameter of 8.00 +/- 0.003 mm. → STE: Ream the hole... (specific, not general)
- Non-STE: Grease the fasteners. → STE: Apply grease to the fasteners.
- Non-STE: Do not enter the engine test area without approval. → STE: Do not go into the engine test area without approval. (but STE: Enter your password. — computer context)

### Rule 1.13 — Do not use technical verbs as nouns.
- Use technical verbs only as verbs.
- Allowed: the past participle form of a technical verb as an adjective ("Lubricate the reamed hole." — "reamed hole" is a technical noun).
- A word can be both a technical verb and a technical noun if it fits categories under both Rules 1.12 and 1.5 (e.g. "plate": part noun vs. "plate the ring nut" surface-finish verb).

Examples:
- Non-STE: Give the hole 0.20-inch ream. → STE: Ream the hole to a 0.20-inch dimension.
- STE: Make sure that the plate is not damaged. (technical noun) / There are two methods to plate the ring nut. (technical verb)

### Rule 1.14 — Use American English spelling unless other official directives tell you differently.
- Use the STE dictionary (American English) spelling; deviate only when technical publication specifications, style guides, contracts, or other official directives apply.
- Exception: never change spelling inside quoted text (e.g. British spelling on a screen or placard) — keep quoted text as it is (see Rule 8.6).

Example:
- Non-STE: Change the colour of the display. → STE: Change the color of the display.

## Section 2 — Multi-word nouns

### Rule 2.1 — Write multi-word nouns of no more than three words.
- Multi-word nouns are groups of nouns/adjectives that act as one part of speech; the head noun is usually last. Long chains are ambiguous, especially for non-native readers.
- Maximum: three words. Break longer chains with prepositions ("of," "on," "in," "for") that make the relations explicit.

Examples:
- Non-STE: Runway light connection resistance calibration. → STE: Calibration of the resistance of the runway light connection.
- Non-STE: Remove the engine transmission housing attachment bolts. → STE: Remove the bolts that attach the transmission housing to the engine.
- Non-STE: Install the forward turbine overheat thermocouple terminal tags. → STE: Install the terminal tags on the forward overheat thermocouple of the turbine.

### Rule 2.2 — When a technical noun has more than three words, write it in full. Then, you can use one of these methods to make the technical noun clear: give a shorter form of the technical noun, or use hyphens (-) between words that you use as one unit.
- Approved technical nouns cannot be split or reworded — write them in their approved form.

Method 1 — Shorter form:
- Write the long official technical noun in full at first occurrence, explain it if possible, then use a shorter form (≤3 words) or an approved abbreviation for the rest of the text.
- Example: "ramp service door safety connector pin (…referred to as the 'safety connector pin')" — then use "safety connector pin".
- Do not abbreviate technical nouns of three words or less (write "diaphragm assembly", not "DA").
- Avoid procedures full of abbreviations — shorter but harder to read.

Method 2 — Hyphens:
- Hyphenate words that operate as one unit; hyphenated words count as one word toward the three-word limit (e.g. "cutoff-switch power connection" = 3 words).
- Only connect words that are truly related — a wrong hyphen changes meaning. If unsure, explain the noun and use a shorter form instead.
- Do not hyphenate everything into one giant unit: Non-STE: main-gear-door-retraction-winch handle → STE: main-gear-door retraction-winch handle.
- Do not add hyphens to approved technical nouns of ≤3 words ("poppet valve assembly", not "poppet-valve assembly").
- If an approved technical noun already includes a hyphen ("inward-outward valve"), never remove it.

## Section 3 — Verbs

### Rule 3.1 — Use only the verb forms that are given in the dictionary.
- The dictionary lists the usable forms for each approved verb, e.g. REMOVE/REMOVES/REMOVED/REMOVED; GIVE/GIVES/GAVE/GIVEN.

### Rule 3.2 — Use only these verb forms and tenses of verbs: the infinitive form, the imperative form (command form), the simple present tense, the simple past tense, the simple future tense, the past participle form (as an adjective).
- Approved: (to) adjust; adjust + object (command); adjust(s); adjusted; will adjust; the adjusted linkage.
- Not approved: present perfect (have/has adjusted), past perfect (had adjusted), present/past progressive (is/was adjusting), and all other complex verb constructions.

### Rule 3.3 — Use the past participle form as an adjective.
- As an adjective, the past participle shows the condition of something — this is not passive voice. Use it: before a noun, or after "to be," "to become," or "to stay."
- Do not use a past participle form that is not in the dictionary.
- Some dictionary adjectives are past participles of unapproved verbs (e.g. "permitted," "damaged") — approved as (adj), so usable.

Examples in STE:
- Examine all parts of the disassembled unit for damage.
- When the unit is fully disassembled, clean all the parts.
- Make sure that the mating surfaces are not damaged.

### Rule 3.4 — Do not use auxiliary verbs to make complex verb constructions.
- Do not combine "have" with a past participle (makes an unapproved tense).
- Do not combine other auxiliaries (is to be, can be, must be, will be) with a past participle — these make complex passive constructions.

Examples:
- Non-STE: The operator has adjusted the linkage. → STE: The operator adjusted the linkage.
- Non-STE: The temperature must be adjusted. → STE: Adjust the temperature.
- Non-STE: The sleeve will be adjusted by the robot. → STE: The robot will adjust the sleeve.

### Rule 3.5 — Use the "-ing" form of a verb only as a technical noun or as a modifier in a technical noun.
- "-ing" words can act as verb parts, adjectives, nouns, or long modifier chains — causing ambiguity — so they are usually not permitted.
- Allowed as technical nouns, e.g. in procedural titles/headings: Cleaning, Testing and Fault Isolation, Handling, Packaging, Shipping, Troubleshooting.
- Allowed as a modifier in a technical noun (adjective related to the function of a system/component/part/tool/material/equipment): air-conditioning system, degreasing agent, grinding wheel, polishing disc, sanding machine, switching relay, welding torch.
- Only a small number of approved dictionary words have an "-ing" form:
  - Nouns: lighting, opening, routing, servicing
  - Adjectives: mating, missing, remaining
  - Pronoun: something
  - Preposition: during

Example:
- Non-STE: When you are doing this procedure, obey all the safety precautions. → STE: When you do this procedure, obey all the safety precautions.

### Rule 3.6 — Use the active voice. In descriptive writing, you can use the passive voice only if the agent is unknown.
- Always use the active voice; passive is permitted only in descriptive writing when the agent (person or thing that does the action) is unknown.
- Passive test: ask "by whom or by what?" — if the text answers it, it is passive. The preposition "by" is a strong indicator; its object is the agent. Passive sentences do not always state an agent ("The dimensions are given in the table.").
- If the true agent is unknown, keep the passive or use "something" as agent — do not invent a technically incorrect agent ("Transmission corrupted the data" is wrong; "During transmission, the data was corrupted" is correct).

Four methods to convert passive → active:
1. If the agent is given (object of "by"), make it the subject: "The circuits are connected by a switching relay." → "A switching relay connects the circuits."
2. Change an infinitive verb to an active verb: "These values are used by the computer to calculate the energy consumption." → "The computer calculates the energy consumption from these values."
3. In procedural writing, use the imperative: "The test can be continued by the operator." → "Continue the test."
4. If no agent is given, use "you" (agent = reader) or "we" (agent = your company): "On the ground, the valve can be opened with the override handle." → "On the ground, you can open the valve with the override handle."

For complex passives with auxiliaries, decide whether the sentence is procedural or descriptive:
- Non-STE: The volume control can be adjusted. → STE (procedural): Adjust the volume control. / STE (descriptive): You can adjust the volume control.
- Non-STE: The oil temperature must be adjusted before the start of the test. → STE (procedural): Before you start the test, adjust the oil temperature. / STE (descriptive): Before the start of the test, the operator must adjust the oil temperature.
- Non-STE: The component is to be installed before you do the test. → STE (procedural): Before you do the test, install the component. / STE (descriptive): Before the test, the operator must install the component.

### Rule 3.7 — Use an approved verb to describe an action, not a noun or other parts of speech.
- If an approved verb describes the action, use it — direct verbs describe actions more clearly than nouns.
- If a word is not approved as a verb, do not use it as a verb; restructure instead (approved noun + approved verb such as "do").

Examples:
- Do not write: The ohmmeter gives an indication of 450 ohms. → WRITE: The ohmmeter shows 450 ohms.
- Do not write: Before the removal of the unit... → WRITE: Before you remove the unit...
- Non-STE: Check the laptop battery. → STE: Do a check of the laptop battery. ("check" not approved as a verb)
