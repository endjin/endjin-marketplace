## Section 7 — Safety instructions

Safety instructions tell readers that procedures or work steps can be dangerous or cause damage. Descriptive writing usually does not include them, unless quoted or the description itself covers safety risks.

Definitions (aerospace/defense usage):
- **Warning** = risk of injury or death.
- **Caution** = risk of damage to objects.

Other industries may use other words ("danger," "attention," "notice") or graphical symbols — that is allowed, but the content must still obey rules 7.1–7.3. (Formatting, e.g. uppercase, is not regulated by STE; follow the applicable specification or style guide.)

### Rule 7.1 — Use an applicable word (for example, "warning" or "caution") to identify the level of risk.

Use a word (or, when applicable, a symbol) that immediately shows the reader the level of risk. Do an accurate risk analysis first, then decide:
- Risk of injury or death → "warning"
- Risk of damage to machines, tools, or equipment → "caution"
- Both levels of risk together → "warning"

Examples in STE:
- `WARNING: BEFORE YOU FILL THE LIQUID OXYGEN SYSTEM, PUT ON A FACE MASK AND PROTECTIVE CLOTHING. LIQUID OXYGEN CAN CAUSE IRRITATION OF THE RESPIRATORY TRACT AND EYE IRRITATION.`
- `CAUTION: BEFORE YOU OPERATE THE GROUND TEST UNIT, MAKE SURE THAT THE PRESSURE REGULATOR IS SET TO ZERO. THIS PRECAUTION WILL PREVENT DAMAGE TO THE UNIT.`

Risk-level example:
- Non-STE: `CAUTION: EXTREME CLEANLINESS OF OXYGEN TUBES IS IMPERATIVE.`
- STE: `WARNING: MAKE SURE THAT THE OXYGEN TUBES ARE FULLY CLEAN. OXYGEN AND GREASE MAKE AN EXPLOSIVE MIXTURE. AN EXPLOSION CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH.`

(The non-STE version is abstract and misclassifies the risk: contaminated oxygen tubes can cause an explosion — injury or death — so it must be a warning, with concrete words like "explosion," "injury," "death" that show its importance.)

### Rule 7.2 — Start a safety instruction with a clear and accurate command or condition.

Start with a clear, accurate command so the reader knows how to prevent accidents and keep a high level of safety:
- `WARNING: DO NOT SWALLOW THE SOLVENT. ALWAYS MAKE SURE THAT YOU KNOW THE SAFETY PRECAUTIONS AND FIRST AID INSTRUCTIONS FOR SOLVENTS. ...`
- `CAUTION: DO NOT USE BLEACH OR CLEANSERS THAT CONTAIN CHLORINE TO CLEAN THE UNIT. ...`

If the reader must know a condition before the procedure or work step starts, give the condition first:
- `WARNING: WHILE YOU USE THE SPRAY PAINT, POINT THE SPRAY AWAY FROM YOUR FACE. ...`
- `CAUTION: WHEN YOU ASSEMBLE THE UNIT, DO NOT LET THE PARTS FALL. ...`

### Rule 7.3 — Give an explanation to show the risk or possible result.

If possible, always tell the reader what can happen if the safety instruction is not obeyed. A clear, specified risk makes the person doing the task more careful. The explanation follows the command/condition:
- `... SOLVENTS ARE POISONOUS AND CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH.`
- `... THESE CLEANING AGENTS CAN CAUSE CORROSION.`
- `... IF THEY FALL, PERMANENT DAMAGE TO THE PARTS CAN OCCUR.`

Pattern of a full safety instruction: **level word (WARNING/CAUTION) + command (condition first if needed) + explanation of the risk or possible result.**

## Section 8 — Punctuation and word count

STE gives no rules for general punctuation (refer to standard style manuals); correct punctuation prevents ambiguity and shows how parts of the text are related. The rules below are STE-specific.

### Rule 8.1 — You can use all standard English punctuation marks but not the semicolon (;).

The semicolon is not permitted: it lets you write very long sentences and is hard to use correctly. Instead, always write two different sentences.

- Non-STE: `Examine the removed parts; replace the damaged ones.`
- STE: `(1) Examine the removed parts for damage. (2) Replace the damaged part(s).`

- Non-STE: `The battery is not user-replaceable; it can only be replaced by an approved service station.`
- STE: `Users cannot replace the battery. Only specialists at approved service stations can replace it.`

### Rule 8.2 — Use hyphens (-) to connect words that are directly related.

Use the hyphen in technical nouns to show that two or more words are directly related. Hyphenate:

1. Multi-word terms used as adjectives before a noun:
   *low-altitude flight, high-pressure chamber, air-conditioned compartment, transmitter-receiver system, quick-release fastener, clamshell-type flap, eighteen-inch monitor, cast-aluminum bracket, three-to-one ratio, trial-and-error method, air-to-air refueling, soap-and-water solution, up-to-date information, run-on torque, break-away torque, cut-in speed, in-flight entertainment system, stiff-bristled brush, fire-resistant material, self-sealing hose*.
2. Two-word fractions or numbers:
   *forty-seven, ninety-ninth, one hundred and sixty-two, three-sixteenths, one thirty-second*.
3. Uppercase letter + noun, or number + noun (usually shape/configuration):
   *L-shaped bracket, O-ring, T-shirt, U-beam, Y-coupling, V-band clamp, 3-prong connector, 180-grit abrasive cloth*.
4. Verbs whose first part is a noun or other part of speech:
   *die-cast, arc-weld, fusion-bond, stop-drill, vacuum-pack, heat-treat, jump-start, air-condition, short-circuit, fast-forward, cold-roll, dry-clean, blow-dry*.
5. Terms where a prefix ending in a vowel meets a root starting with a vowel:
   *pre-amplifier, de-icing, anti-icing, pre-engage*.

Note: a hyphen is not a dash. A dash divides ideas, shows a range, or signals a pause.

### Rule 8.3 — You can use parentheses:

Permitted uses (with example each):
1. References to illustrations or text: `Remove the valve (10, Figure 1).` / `Install the cover (refer to paragraphs 2 thru 5).`
2. Letters/numbers that identify items on an illustration or in text: `Disconnect the hoses (2) and (12) from the suction ejector (8).`
3. Identifying work steps in a procedure: `(1) Install the locking cap (4) on the body (8). (2) Safety the locking cap (4) with the cotter pin (5).`
4. Abbreviations: `A Liquid Crystal Display (LCD) is a flat-panel display ...`
5. Singular and plural forms at the same time: `Do the applicable test(s).`
6. Explaining words or part of a sentence: `Increase the pressure slowly (not more than 10 psi each minute).`
7. Including an alternative: `Open the left (right) access panel L42 (R42).`

### Rule 8.4 — In a vertical list, a colon (:) has the same effect on word count as a period and shows the end of a sentence.

The colon divides the lead-in from the list items and acts as a period. Therefore:
- Max **20 words** before the colon in procedural sentences; max **25 words** in descriptive sentences.
- Each item after the colon counts as a **new sentence** with the same limits: 20 words (procedural) / 25 words (descriptive).

Example:
```
To extinguish a possible fire, portable fire extinguishers are installed
in these areas:                       (13 words)
  - The cockpit                        (2 words)
  - The cabin                          (2 words)
  - The cabin sub-compartment          (3 words)
  - The crew rest compartment.         (4 words)
```

### Rule 8.5 — When you put text in parentheses, it counts as one word in that sentence.

Text in parentheses = one word of the containing sentence, but the parenthetical text is also a new sentence — count its words separately against the limit.

- `Make sure that the EMER pushbutton switch is released (the EMER legend is off).` = 10 words; the parenthetical sentence itself has 5 words.

Identifiers in parentheses (a number, letter, or alphanumeric identifier) and abbreviations in parentheses each count as one word:
- `Remove the safety pin (10).` (5 words)
- `Installation of a Business Class (B/C) Seat` (7 words)

### Rule 8.6 — Count each of these elements as one word: numbers; numbers together with units of measurement; abbreviations; alphanumeric identifiers; quoted text; titles, headings, and text on placards and labels; proper nouns of individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities.

1. **Numbers**: `Do steps 13 thru 16 a minimum of three times.` (10 words); `The spar box has twenty-one ribs.` (6 words). Do not count numbers that identify paragraphs or work steps (document numbering).
2. **Number + unit of measurement** = one word: `The unit weighs 20 kg.` (4 words); `Make sure that the temperature in the room is 10 °C.` (10 words).
3. **Abbreviations** (also acronyms and initialisms) = one word each: `During this safety check, obey NASA protocols.` (7 words); `... each day at 10 a.m.` ("10 a.m." counts as one word).
4. **Alphanumeric identifiers** = one word: `Tag circuit breaker 36L7.` (4 words); `Examine the No. 1 bearing installation.` (5 words — "No. 1" is one word).
5. **Quoted text** = one word (whether in quotation marks, or shown by uppercase/font): `Touch the "Service Overview" arrow to select the function page.` (9 words); `Release the SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST switch.` (4 words). A formula is quoted text: `C = (A - B) - 0.063 mm` (1 word).
6. **Titles, headings, placard and label text** — words you cannot change = one word:
   - `Refer to Testing and Fault Isolation, page block 1001.` (6 words — the section title counts as one word)
   - `Before you start a repair, refer to the Structural Repair Manual for the applicable safety procedures and precautions.` (16 words)
   - A full quoted placard or label text (e.g., `"FRAGILE - Please handle with care."`) = 1 word, even when it is not in STE, because you cannot change it.
   - A reference number plus a quoted unchangeable title each count as one word: `This procedure is for the inspection of SSI No. 57-21-16, "Outer wing bottom skin ..."` = 9 words (7 + "SSI No. 57-21-16" as 1 + the quoted SSI title as 1).
7. **Proper nouns** of individuals, groups, organizations, geopolitical entities = one word each: `The first president of the United States of America was George Washington.` (8 words).

### Rule 8.7 — Hyphenated words count as one word.

Hyphenated word groups that function as an adjective before a noun count as one word, and so do hyphenated long technical nouns:
- `Clean the surface with a soap-and-water solution.` (7 words)
- `Use the trial-and-error method.` (4 words)
- `Main-gear-door retraction-winch handle` (3 words)

## Section 9 — Writing practices

### Rule 9.1 — Use a different sentence construction to write a sentence when a word-for-word replacement is not sufficient.

The dictionary gives approved alternatives for unapproved words. If the alternative has the same part of speech and keeps the meaning, use a word-for-word replacement:
- Non-STE: `A value of 2 mm is acceptable.` → STE: `A value of 2 mm is permitted.`

Use a **different sentence construction** when:
1. The alternative forces a change of grammatical structure:
   - Non-STE: `The oil level on the sight gauge must be visible during the test.` → STE: `During the test, make sure that you can see the oil level on the sight gauge.` (verb "see" replaces adjective "visible")
   - Non-STE: `Cycle the unit twice to remove air from the lines.` → STE: `Operate the unit for two cycles to remove air from the lines.`
2. Word-for-word replacement gives a meaningless result:
   - Non-STE: `Without this modification, the service life of the unit can be uncertain.` → STE: `Without this modification, it is possible that the service life of this unit will be shorter than usual.` (not "cannot be sure/known" — not good English)
3. The approved alternative changes the meaning:
   - Non-STE: `Just apply very light pressure.` → STE: `Only apply very light pressure.` NOT `Immediately apply ...` ("immediately" is the dictionary alternative for "just" but changes the meaning here)
4. The word to replace is not in the dictionary at all — think of the meaning and rewrite:
   - Non-STE: `The incidence of water in fuel is dangerous.` → STE: `Water in fuel is dangerous.`

Method: ask "What does this word mean in this context?" and "What action must the reader do?", then rewrite with approved words:
- Non-STE: `Lift the seat so that it clears the track locks.` → STE: `Lift the seat until it is away from the track locks.` NOT `... so that it cleans the track locks.`

You may need to select different words, use different verb forms, write new constructions, divide long sentences, remove unnecessary information, or get more information from an engineer. When you change one part of a text, make sure it has no negative effects on other parts. Information already in a table need not be repeated in the sentence — an instruction can move into a table heading:
- Non-STE: `If cracks are detected during this procedure, the operator must perform the repair within a certain number of flight hours depending on crack length. Refer to following table:` (24 words, passive voice, missing articles, not imperative)
- Simple word replacements ("perform"→"do", "within"→"in", "certain"→"some", "detect"→"find") plus the missing articles still give bad English at 23 words with unclear technical meaning.
- STE: `If you find cracks, refer to the table that follows:` with table headings `If the crack is of this length | Do the repair before`

The primary objective of STE: readers immediately understand each sentence they read.

### Rule 9.2 — Use each approved word correctly.

Approved words usually have only **one approved meaning** (often a restricted meaning); other standard-English meanings are not approved. Read the approved-meaning column before you use a word.

- Non-STE: `Wear protective clothing.` → STE: `Use (or put on) protective clothing.` ("wear" is approved only as "to become damaged by friction")
- Non-STE: `When the pressure goes down, lift the cover.` → STE: `When the pressure decreases, lift the cover.` ("go down" = physical movement of an indicator only)
- Non-STE: `The indicator turns green.` → STE: `The color of the indicator changes to green.` ("turn" = move around an axis or point only)

Other restricted-meaning traps:
- "extend" = increase in dimension or range only: Non-STE `This regulation extends to all units.` → STE `This regulation is applicable to all units.`
- "go through" = physical movement through something only, not a requirement/condition: Non-STE `Make sure that the probe went through more than 500 °C.` → STE `Make sure that the temperature of the probe increased to more than 500 °C.`
- "see" = with the eyes only, not "come to know": Non-STE `Move the tube to see if the inner connection is tight.` → STE `Move the tube to make sure that the inner connection is tight.`
- "above"/"below" = physical positions only, not limits: Non-STE `Do not let the pressure go below (or above) 20 psi.` → STE `Do not let the pressure become less than (or more than) 20 psi.`

Also use each approved word only as its **approved part of speech**:
- Non-STE: `When you work with cleaning agents ...` → STE: `When you do work with cleaning agents ...` ("work" approved as noun only)
- Non-STE: `Install the cover with the help of a second person.` → STE: `... with the aid of a second person.` ("help" approved as verb only)
- Non-STE: `Be careful not to damage the sleeve.` → STE: `Be careful not to cause damage to the sleeve.` ("damage" approved as noun only)

A small number of words are approved as more than one part of speech with more than one meaning (e.g., "flush": verb = clean with a flow of liquid; adjective = surfaces fully touching): `Flush the pipes with a disinfectant solution.` / `Make sure that the door is flush with the adjacent surfaces.` Position and context make the function clear.

### Rule 9.3 — When you use two words together, do not make phrasal verbs.

It is not permitted to combine approved words into a phrasal verb whose meaning differs from the approved meanings of its parts (phrasal verbs are ambiguous: concrete vs. abstract meaning, e.g., "put out the cat" vs. "put out the fire").

- Non-STE: `After you put out the fire, close the valve on the fire extinguisher.` → STE: `After you extinguish the fire, ...`
- Non-STE: `This compound can give off poisonous fumes.` → STE: `This compound can release poisonous fumes.`

Phrasal verbs are not usually listed as "not approved" in the dictionary — you must check that each word is used with its dictionary meaning. Only a small number of phrasal verbs (for example, "put on" and "come on") are approved, all with restricted meanings.

### Rule 9.4 — When you select terminology or wording, always use a consistent style.

For recurring information (especially recurring work-step types), select one terminology/wording and use it every time the context and meaning are the same. Two sentences can both be correct STE, but mixing them for the same instruction causes confusion and delays:
- `Lubricate the two bolts with a small quantity of oil.` vs. `Apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the two bolts.` — pick one.

In a procedure, each item must keep the same noun throughout, and the same action must keep the same wording. Example (condensed from an 8-step procedure):
- Non-STE: step 1 `... on the main body (9).` / step 3 `... in the body (9).` / step 5 `... on the body assembly (9).`; step 4 `Torque-tighten the two bolts to 4 Nm.` / step 8 `Torque each of the four bolts (12) to 0.8 Nm.`; step 2 `Lubricate the two bolts (10) with oil.` / step 6 `Apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the four bolts (12).`
- STE: always `body assembly (9)`, always `Torque each of the ... bolts to ... Nm.`, always `Apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the ... bolts ...`.

In descriptive writing, the reader must understand the text as a unit: use words and sentences correctly and give the text a logical structure.

## General recommendations (GR-1 to GR-8) — not STE rules, but prevent typical errors

**GR-1 The conjunction "that":** Use "that" as much as possible after verbs like "make sure," "show," "recommend" — it marks where the main clause ends and the subordinate clause starts, and it helps translation (many languages cannot omit the equivalent word).
- Do not write: `Make sure the valve is open.` → Write: `Make sure that the valve is open.`
- Do not write: `The gauge shows the reservoir is full.` → Write: `The gauge shows that the reservoir is full.`

**GR-2 The preposition "with":** "With" (association/relationship, help/sharing, means/instrument) can be ambiguous — reread the sentence.
- Do not write: `Lift the aircraft at the maximum takeoff weight with passengers.` → Write: `Lift the aircraft at the maximum takeoff weight (passenger weight included).`
- Put a condition first: `When you supply hydraulic pressure, make sure that the lever does not touch the stop (1).`
- Keep the primary action verb: write `Seal the opening with tool TS9867.`, not `Use tool TS9867 to seal the opening.`

**GR-3 Pronouns:** Only pronouns in the dictionary are allowed (not "he"/"she"). If a pronoun could refer to more than one noun, replace it with the noun:
- Do not write: `If you engage the pins incorrectly with the seats, they can become damaged.` → Write: `... the pins can become damaged.` (or "the seats", or "the pins and seats")

**GR-4 The pronoun "this":** If "this" can refer to more than one item, restate the context:
- Do not write: `Make sure that the cover is not locked (this can cause damage to the probe).` → Write: `Make sure that the cover is not locked. If the cover is locked, this can cause damage to the probe.` (Or: `If the cover is locked, damage to the probe can occur.`)
- Do not write: `Do not use crocus cloth on aluminum parts. If you do this, you can cause corrosion ...` → Write: `Do not use crocus cloth on aluminum parts. Crocus cloth contains ferrous oxide, which can cause corrosion on aluminum parts.`

**GR-5 False friends:** Make sure a word has its correct English meaning, not the meaning of a similar-looking word in your native language:
- Do not write: `Obey the dispositions of the manufacturer ...` → Write: `... obey the manufacturer's instructions.`

**GR-6 Latin abbreviations:** Do not use Latin abbreviations ("e.g." = "for example," "i.e." = "that is," "etc." = "and so on") — they confuse readers who do not know them. Use English words, or omit the abbreviation when it is not necessary:
- Do not write: `Discard the standard parts (e.g., washers, screws, bolts, and nuts) each time you remove them.` → Write: `Discard the standard parts (for example, washers, bolts, and nuts) each time you remove them.`
- Do not write: `... colors (blue, green, red, etc.).` → Write: `These wires can have insulation of different colors. They can be blue, green, red, or other colors.` (Or simply: `These wires can have insulation of different colors.`)

**GR-7 Inclusive language:** Always use gender-neutral language. Gender-specific pronouns ("he," "she") are not permitted; "man" and "woman" are not permitted unless necessary in context (e.g., a medical text).

**GR-8 Possessive form:** The possessive ('s) is permitted, but use it correctly; if not sure, do not use it (it is hard for many non-native readers). Follow applicable specifications and style guides.
